# Reading the Gap, Unit Syllabus

One unit in the M.A. in Artificial Intelligence Innovation.

This is one unit inside a three-credit, seven-week LifeFlex course, which would hold around six or seven units across the term. The unit teaches one idea, the gap between what you mean by your own images and what an AI makes of them, and it builds that idea across three short lessons, one hands-on activity, and one assessment. Everything stays asynchronous and self-paced, with the optional live session noted in the assessment.

---

## The idea this unit teaches

There is no single correct reading of an image, only readings from a position. When you hand your own screenshots to an AI, it returns a reading from its position, and the distance between that and what you meant is the gap. This unit treats the gap as the thing to learn from, not an error to fix. Noticing where the tool's reading parts from your intention is information about the tool and about yourself, and that noticing is the skill the unit builds.

---

## What you can do by the end

1. Find the places where an AI's reading of your own images parts from what you meant by them.
2. Tell apart what you can point to, what you are assuming, and what you cannot explain, using your own work as the evidence.
3. Read your own use of an AI tool through the four relations from the lectures, and work out the conditions under which the tool would slip out of sight for you.
4. Judge, from your own experience, whether this way of working is worth keeping.

---

## How the unit is built

Three lessons, one continuous nine-stage activity that runs through them, and an assessment that caps the unit. The activity is a single arc in one workspace, and its stages are spread across the three lessons, so each lesson has its own video, its own hands-on doing, and its own readings. The lessons move in order, set your own meaning first, then watch the gap open, then turn the lens on yourself.

### The nine stages, mapped to the three lessons

| Lesson | Stages | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Setting your meaning first | 1, 2 | Build your coaching agent, then write down what your own screenshots mean to you and save it before any AI reads them. |
| 2. The gap appears (recorded video) | 3, 4, 5, 6 | The model reads your archive, you test what it can point to versus what it guessed, a second agent sorts by why you saved each shot, and the image generator returns a picture you compare. |
| 3. Reading yourself | 7, 8, 9 | The 2D/3D site, the master prompt, and the coach reading your whole prompt record back to you. |

## Lesson 1, Setting your meaning first

Stages 1 and 2.

Video synopsis, about five minutes. The video opens on the problem, that we let tools read our images before we have said what we meant, so we cannot tell afterward what the tool changed. It introduces the four relations from philosophy of technology as the language the unit will use, hermeneutic, alterity, background, embodiment, one plain line each. Then it shows the move of this lesson, building a small coaching agent that carries your own way of reading, and writing down what each of your screenshots means to you and why you saved it, saved as `my-interpretation.md` before any AI touches them. It closes by naming that this saved file is the recorded, unedited statement of your own meaning, the fixed point the rest of the unit measures the gap against.

Readings, AI and methodology and digital humanities. This lesson's readings ground how digital humanities work treats AI as something to study and interpret rather than simply use, and how method shapes what you can claim. Likely anchors, to confirm before publishing: Lucy Suchman on human-machine relations, Nick Seaver on studying algorithms as culture, plus a digital-humanities method reading you choose. ## Lesson 2, The gap appears

Stages 3 through 6. This is the recorded video.

Video synopsis, about five to ten minutes. The video does two jobs at once, which is why it sits in the middle and touches both directions.

Looking back, it reviews lesson one. It recalls the coaching agent you built and the philosophy-of-technology language of the four relations, so a student returning to it is reminded of the stance they set before any tool spoke, and of the saved meaning they will now read the gap against.

Looking forward, it sets up the rest of the unit. It shows the gap opening across several tools in turn, the model's confident sort of your archive, the type it names but cannot point to on the screen, the second agent splitting your shots by a different logic, and the image that adds or drops things you never asked for. Then it names gap-reading as the single move the whole unit turns on, the move used in lesson one to set your meaning, here in lesson two to see the gap open, and in lesson three to read yourself. So the video justifies its own placement, it is both the review of where you started and the hinge into where the unit goes.

Readings, postphenomenology. This is where the relations vocabulary is read closely, so the four relations stop being labels and become a way to describe your own stance toward a tool. Anchors, to confirm before publishing: Don Ihde on the human-technology relations, Peter-Paul Verbeek on mediation, and your own published work on AI-mediated images. Add exact citations here.

## Lesson 3, Reading yourself

Stages 7 through 9.

Video synopsis, about five to seven minutes. The video opens on the turn, that the unit has been about the tools, and now it turns to you. It shows the master prompt, where you carry your own saved meaning and the tools' readings into one place, and then the coach reading your whole prompt record back to you, naming how you were relating to the tools across the session. It closes on the reflexive point, that the goal was never a correct reading of your archive, it was learning to watch your own reading, which is what the assessment then asks you to do.

Readings, screenshot writings, chosen to aid reflection on your own media. Because the student's material is their own phone screenshots, this lesson's readings sit closest to that specific medium, how screenshots carry meaning, how saving one is already an act of interpretation, and how that meaning shifts when a tool reads it back. These align with the earlier two lessons by turning their method and their relations vocabulary onto the exact thing the student is holding. Anchors, to confirm before publishing: your own screenshot and social-media interpretation work, plus one reading on vernacular image practices you choose. Add exact citations here.
